The Drunkard's Walk

by Leonard Mlodinow

I think I was far from the target audience for this book - essentially a gentle introduction to how you can use probability, statistics and Bayesian thinking in everyday life for laypeople.

Nonetheless, I found it entertaining and informative. Mlodinow shows more personality than most, and the book is littered with anecdotes and clever jokes.

The Drunkard's Walk also hammers home some good points, which stuck with me the past days. One is that we're fundamentally pattern-seeking creatures. A necessary survival tool in the prehistoric savannah, but easily abused in today's complex random world. Most people tend to think they're special, or that they can control/predict something random, or simply don't have any probabilistic intuition. In sports, I still fall victim to the hot hand fallacy, call players streaky, and try to post-rationalise why a team is on a run.

Expectations feed reality. We think people do a better job if they're paid more, even if we know that the pay was distributed randomly (pg 210). "Dress for success" and "fake it til you make it" are real. Our brain genuinely perceives expensive or highly-rated wines as better, when blind studies show uniform distributions (pg 132).

Our brain has a strong tendency to "other". We have less empathy for people who seem less relatable, and have unconscious biases against those in the bottom of society. We don't see the failures of the homeless or the success of the rich as random fluctuations or luck, because we're scared to give up control over our own "life paths" (whatever that bullshit means).

Our need for control is real and but also practical. In a nursing home, residents who could make small changes in their room were happier and lived longer (pg 186). We're more attached to things if we got to pick it out. I guess it's another argument that agency is a fundamental human need, and might support libertarianism.

I really liked his explanation of how the central limit theorem applies to measurement errors. A myriad of small deviations compound in random directions, which is just a Bernoulli process, so errors follow the normal distribution under the law of large numbers (pg 144).

Mlodinow also explains the Monty Hall problem, law of large numbers, and false positives very well. The latter is especially important to be aware of if the doctor ever gives someone scary test results.

Jacob Bernoulli might be a GOAT. Stock brokers and wine connoisseurs are charlatans.

Overall a very easy guide to getting a better foundation for everyday mathematical intuitions. I'd it recommend anyone who wants to become more aware of the fallacies they fall victim to. But Lenny, I'd like to see you use your precious "statistics" and so-called "probability" to explain the Maple Leafs.
